## Title and Authors of the Paper

*Climate change causes critical transitions and irreversible alterations of mountain forests* — Katharina Albrich, Werner Rammer, and Rupert Seidl (2019), Global Change Biology. Affiliation: Institute of Silviculture, BOKU Vienna; Ecosystem Dynamics and Forest Management Group, TU Munich.

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** Whether climate change can trigger irreversible, threshold-type transitions in mountain forest ecosystems remains poorly understood, with major implications for biodiversity, ecosystem services, and management.
- **What was done?** The individual-based forest landscape model iLand was used to simulate forest responses to a wide range of climate forcing scenarios (up to +6°C warming, −30% precipitation) in the Eastern Alps, testing for tipping points and reversibility.
- **What is the main outcome?** A clear tipping point was identified at +2°C mean annual warming, above which the system shifts irreversibly from a conifer-dominated large-tree landscape to a smaller, predominantly broadleaved forest, with topographic complexity acting as a key buffer.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

The research investigates whether progressive climate change can push mountain forest ecosystems across critical thresholds — tipping points — beyond which the system enters an alternative stable state. A secondary question is whether these transitions are reversible if the climate forcing is subsequently reduced. The study focuses on two ecologically and economically important attributes of Alpine mountain forests: species composition (Norway spruce dominance) and size structure (density of large-diameter trees). These attributes underpin key ecosystem services including timber production, protection against natural hazards, and carbon storage.

## Technical Approach

The study uses iLand (individual-based forest landscape and disturbance model), a spatially explicit, process-based simulation model that tracks individual trees and simulates growth, mortality, and regeneration at annual time steps. Productivity is calculated monthly using a resource use efficiency approach, driven by daily climate variables (temperature, precipitation, radiation, vapour pressure deficit) and soil properties. Regeneration is simulated at 4 m² grain cells; water balance at 1 ha cells. The 4,811 ha Stubai Valley study landscape was simulated under a full factorial climate forcing design: mean annual temperature increased from +0°C to +6°C in 1°C steps, crossed with mean annual precipitation reductions of 0%, 15%, and 30%. Each climate state was held constant for 1,000 years to allow equilibrium analysis, removing transient land-use legacies. Three topography scenarios (complex, intermediate, uniform) were used to isolate the effect of topographic heterogeneity. To test reversibility, warming trajectories were reversed (+6°C to +0°C) and compared to warming trajectories using Bray-Curtis Dissimilarity and PERMANOVA.

## Distinctive Features

The paper's most distinctive contribution is the combination of equilibrium analysis (eliminating land-use legacies and transient dynamics) with explicit reversibility testing — simulating both warming and cooling trajectories to detect hysteresis. This design is unusual in forest ecology and allows for the formal identification of alternative stable states and irreversibility thresholds. The three topography scenarios additionally enable a rigorous, mechanistic analysis of topographic buffering that goes beyond correlative landscape-level analyses.

## Experimental Setup and Results

The focal landscape is the Stubai Valley (47.10°N, 11.29°E), a 4,811 ha conifer-dominated forest landscape spanning an elevational gradient from 900 m to 2,000 m a.s.l. (timberline). Current mean annual temperature is 4.1°C, mean annual precipitation 998 mm. Dominant species: Norway spruce (*Picea abies*), European larch (*Larix decidua*), Swiss stone pine (*Pinus cembra*).

Key results:
- A nonlinear tipping point was identified at approximately +2°C warming: above this threshold the landscape shifted from Norway spruce dominance and high large-tree density to a landscape of smaller, predominantly broadleaved trees.
- Topographic complexity (complex > intermediate > uniform) smoothed and delayed — but did not prevent — the transition at higher warming levels.
- When climate forcing was reversed (cooling from +6°C to +0°C), the system did not return to its original state (hysteresis), particularly in low-precipitation scenarios.
- At identical mean annual temperatures, equilibrium vegetation differed between warming and cooling trajectories — the system had entered an alternative stable attractor.
- Irreversibility persisted at millennial timescales once the tipping point was exceeded.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages:** The equilibrium design provides a clear mechanistic picture of alternative stable states unconfounded by land-use legacies. Explicit reversibility testing is methodologically rigorous and ecologically informative. The iLand model has been extensively validated for Central European forest landscapes. Results are directly policy-relevant, linking warming thresholds to the +1.5–2°C Paris Agreement targets.

**Limitations:** Equilibrium analysis by definition excludes disturbance dynamics (fire, windstorm, bark beetle) which could drastically accelerate or modify transition pathways in practice. The model does not account for evolutionary adaptation of tree populations to novel climates, potentially underestimating resilience. A single study landscape limits transferability to other Alpine or mountain forest contexts. Soil properties were held static throughout simulations, ignoring potential soil–vegetation feedbacks.

## Conclusion

Albrich et al. (2019) demonstrate that even moderate warming — at or slightly above current Paris Agreement targets — can push Eastern Alpine mountain forests across a critical, largely irreversible threshold. The shift from conifer-dominated old-growth forest to a shorter, broadleaf-dominated system represents a fundamental reorganisation of ecosystem structure and function. Topographic complexity buffers but cannot prevent these transitions under high warming. The demonstration of millennial-scale irreversibility makes this one of the most consequential findings for long-term forest management and conservation planning in European mountain regions.
