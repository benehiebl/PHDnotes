## Title and Authors of the Paper

*Native Trees of the Mediterranean Region: Distribution, Diversity and Conservation Challenges* — Bruno Fady, Anna-Maria Farsakoglou, Mercedes Caron, and 27 co-authors (2025), Current Forestry Reports. Affiliations include INRAE (France), European Forest Institute (Barcelona), and institutions from Jordan, Serbia, Iran, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Egypt, Israel, Turkey, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Iraq, Belgium, Russia, and Lebanon.

## Quick Overview

- **Why is it relevant?** A comprehensive, up-to-date inventory of native tree species in the Mediterranean biodiversity hotspot — covering presence/absence, extinction risk, and genetic diversity — has been lacking despite the region's disproportionate global importance for tree biodiversity.
- **What was done?** A collaborative, multi-institutional database was compiled providing presence/absence, extinction risk, biogeographic, and genetic diversity data for trees across 39 ecologically and climatically defined Mediterranean botanical territories in North Africa, Western Asia, and Southern Europe.
- **What is the main outcome?** The inventory documents 496 native tree species and 147 subspecies, reveals major endemism and extinction-risk hotspots, and identifies critical knowledge gaps — particularly in genetic diversity data — that constrain conservation planning across the region.

## Main Goal and Fundamental Concept

The study addresses a fundamental knowledge gap: the absence of a consistent, ecologically grounded inventory of native tree species across the entire Mediterranean Basin at a resolution useful for conservation and forest management. While 38% of tree species globally are at risk of extinction, assessments of Mediterranean tree diversity have been patchy, taxonomically inconsistent, and politically fragmented. By defining the inventory around 39 "botanical territories" — units defined by climate and vegetation rather than national borders — the study provides a spatially coherent framework for characterising tree distribution, diversity, endemism, and threat status across the region's three continents.

## Technical Approach

An international network of botanists and forest ecologists — covering 39 Mediterranean botanical territories across Southern Europe (including Iberian Peninsula, France, Italian Peninsula, Balkans, Greece, Cyprus, Turkey), North Africa (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt), and Western Asia (Lebanon, Syria, Palestine, Jordan, Iraq, Iran) — compiled a presence/absence database of native tree species for each territory. The definition of "tree" was applied consistently, following the framework of Médail et al. (2019). Each species was assessed for: (1) botanical territory-level occurrence (presence/absence), (2) IUCN extinction risk category (from the European Red List of Trees and national assessments), and (3) availability of genetic diversity data in the literature. Species richness was modelled against botanical territory area and habitat heterogeneity metrics. Correlations between richness (raw and area-standardised) and geographic/environmental predictors were computed. The full database has been made open access to support research and conservation applications.

## Distinctive Features

The study is the first comprehensive, multi-territory, open-access inventory of Mediterranean native trees covering all three continents of the basin, built on a botanically and ecologically consistent framework. It goes beyond floristic catalogues by integrating extinction risk and genetic diversity assessments, explicitly mapping knowledge gaps rather than just documenting what is known. The identification of 48 species and 8 subspecies previously not recognised as trees represents a taxonomic contribution that expands the scope of future monitoring and conservation programmes.

## Experimental Setup and Results

The inventory covers 39 botanical territories. Key quantitative findings:
- 496 native tree species and 147 subspecies from 50 families and 111 genera
- 48 species and 8 subspecies newly recognised as trees
- Species distribution strongly skewed: Rosaceae is the dominant family; most families contain <1% of all species
- Endemism is unevenly distributed across botanical territories (islands and southern mountain ranges show particularly high endemism)
- Extinction risk assessment is lacking for almost half of inventoried species
- Species richness is positively correlated with botanical territory area; area-standardised richness is positively correlated with habitat heterogeneity
- Genetic diversity information is absent for approximately two-thirds of inventoried species; available data are biased towards economically important species (e.g., oaks, conifers)
- Major tree richness hotspots identified: Balkans, Turkey, and mountainous zones of North Africa

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages:** The cross-continental, territory-based approach overcomes the political fragmentation that has limited previous regional inventories. The open-access database provides a reusable resource for conservation planning, trait-based ecology, and genetic studies. Explicit mapping of knowledge gaps is as valuable as the positive records. The identification of hotspots provides immediate conservation prioritisation guidance.

**Limitations:** Presence/absence data do not capture abundance, population size, or demographic status — all critical for extinction risk assessment. IUCN assessments are incomplete for nearly half the species, limiting the power of threat analyses. Genetic diversity data are highly biased toward commercially important species, leaving most of the tree flora in a data void. Botanical territory definitions, while ecologically meaningful, do not align with administrative units used in national forest policies, potentially limiting direct policy uptake.

## Conclusion

Fady et al. (2025) deliver the most comprehensive inventory of Mediterranean native trees to date, revealing a region that is simultaneously species-rich, highly endemic, and poorly characterised for conservation purposes. The positive correlation of tree richness with habitat heterogeneity underscores the importance of topographically and ecologically complex landscapes for maintaining tree diversity under future climate change. The study's most urgent message is quantitative: large fractions of Mediterranean tree species lack even basic extinction risk and genetic diversity assessments — a gap that must be addressed as climate change accelerates range shifts and local extinctions across the basin.
